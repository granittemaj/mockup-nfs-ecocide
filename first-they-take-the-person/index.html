Forced labour and environmental crime

First they take the person. Then they take the planet.

The people destroying the planet are not who you think. Behind the shovel, the saw and the flame stands a system that profits from human and environmental exploitation at once, and stays invisible in both.

The damage looks easy to explain. A poisoned river. A forest cut back to mud. An illegal mine tearing open land that should have remained untouched. We look for the person holding the chainsaw, shovel or flame and assume we have found the culprit.

But what if the hand doing the damage is also being controlled?

This is the connection Not For Sale founder Mark Wexler believes the world has failed to see. Across illegal mining, logging and other extractive economies, environmental destruction can be driven by people trapped through debt, threats, coercion or conditions they cannot leave.

The worker is visible, while the system profiting from human and environmental exploitation remains hidden. What follows is an inventory of what that worker actually owns.

Act one · Ownership

What the worker does not have

Every element of the operation belongs to someone else. Five things, none of them theirs. The ledger keeps count.

01 — The site

They do not own the mines.

  1. The concession holder, where one exists on paper
  2. The financier who paid for pumps, hoses and fuel
  3. The site boss who decides who works and for how long

Roles, not named individuals. Which of them exists on paper varies by jurisdiction.

The tunnel is dug by hand. The concession, where one exists on paper at all, is held elsewhere.

02 — The forest

They do not own the forests.

  1. The permit holder named on the licence
  2. The buyer who commissioned the cut
  3. The exporter whose licence moves it across the border

The person holding the saw appears on none of these documents.

The permit, the buyer and the export licence rarely name the person holding the saw.

03 — The land

They do not control the land.

  1. The registered title holder
  2. The authority that granted the concession over it
  3. The land registry that records neither the worker nor, often, the community

Land tenure is the first thing lost in an extractive economy.

Land tenure is the first thing lost in an extractive economy, and the last thing returned.

04 — The sale

They do not sell what they extract.

  1. The site buyer who operates the scale
  2. The trader who sets the price before the sack arrives
  3. The exporter who consolidates it with everyone else's

The weight is recorded by someone else's pen.

The weight is recorded by someone else's pen. The price is set before the sack reaches the scale.

05 — The margin

They do not keep the profits.

  1. The trading house that aggregates the volume
  2. The refinery that removes the origin
  3. The market that buys it with no view of the pit

Each step upstream keeps more and knows less.

Value accumulates upstream, in trading houses and refineries far from the mud road.

Act two · Agency

And yet the damage happens

Three verbs. Three visible acts of destruction, each performed by the person with the least power in the chain.

06 — Dig

Yet they dig.

Pits are opened by hand and by hose, without survey, shoring or any plan for closing them again.

07 — Burn

They burn.

Kilns and clearance fires turn standing forest into transportable fuel. Charcoal is itself a listed good.

08 — Poison

They poison.

Tailings and process water leave the site by the shortest route available, which is almost always the river.

The reframe

Not because they choose to.

Because someone else has taken that choice away.

Many are held through debt, threats, coercion or conditions they cannot leave.

The argument

This is not slavery beside ecocide.

This is slavery inside ecocide.

The distinction is not rhetorical. It decides which agency owns the problem, which budget pays for it, and whether the person standing in the water is treated as an offender or as evidence.

Evidence · Two economies, one set of accounts

Measured apart, operated together

Forced labour and environmental crime are counted, funded and legislated separately. Placed side by side they describe the same operation.

Act three · Downstream

The damage returns as recruitment

Follow the contamination out of the pit and watch it turn back into a supply of people.

09 — Release

Illegal mining sends toxic metals into rivers.

Mercury used to separate gold from ore enters the watercourse with the tailings.

10 — Transport

Those rivers carry the pollution across borders.

Rivers do not stop at jurisdictions. Neither, in practice, does the liability settle in any of them.

11 — Collapse

When farms fail, families become more vulnerable.

Contaminated water and degraded soil remove the household income that kept a family in place.

12 — Recruitment

The destruction creates the conditions for more exploitation.

Displacement is the recruiter's raw material. The loop closes here, and then it runs again.

What this changes

We have been looking at the hands.

The person causing the visible damage may be the least powerful person in the chain. Environmental enforcement that targets the visible worker punishes the victim and leaves the operation intact. Anti-trafficking work that ignores the extractive economy misses who is paying for the coercion. The same operation is being half-policed from two directions.
